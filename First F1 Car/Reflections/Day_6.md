## 🛠️ Skills & Techniques Learned
- `Isolate` — Temporarily hiding other bodies to focus on individual components during modeling.
- `Boundary Surface` — Creating complex curved surfaces by controlling multiple guide curves and boundary conditions.
- `Fill Surface` — Closing gaps or creating smooth surfaces between existing geometry.
- `Move/Copy Body` — Repositioning and duplicating solid bodies efficiently.
- `Cut Sweep` — Removing material along a defined path using a profile and guide curve.
- `Split Line` — Creating sketch references or dividing faces for additional features and design details.
- `Insert Text` — Adding text or logos onto surfaces for future livery designs.
- `Advanced Filleting` — Understanding more efficient methods of applying fillets to repeated geometry.

## 💭 Reflection

Today's session was extremely `productive and rewarding`. Although the individual modeling steps were not as difficult as some of the challenges I encountered earlier in the project, I `learned several new techniques` that will definitely be useful for future SolidWorks projects. More importantly, I began `applying my own engineering judgment to improve the model instead of simply reproducing the tutorial`.

The session began with the wheel hubs and suspension wishbones. These components were relatively straightforward because they mainly involved fundamental sketching, extrusion, and cutting operations. However, I learned an important workflow technique during this stage: `the Isolate feature`. By isolating the newly created bodies, I was able to `focus on the individual components` without being distracted by the rest of the model. This made it significantly easier to create accurate sketches and inspect the geometry. Although aligning the wishbones with the main chassis required some adjustments, the overall process was smooth and showed how much more comfortable I have become working with SolidWorks compared to the beginning of the project.

The next stage involved creating the cockpit seat, floor assembly, and engine intake sweep. The cockpit seat and floor were relatively simple because they mainly involved basic extrusion techniques. However, the engine intake introduced me to a feature I had not previously used, which was the Cut Sweep. While the concept was unfamiliar initially, I quickly understood that the feature was useful for removing material along a controlled path. It was a simple but powerful technique that I can see myself using in future projects when creating more complex mechanical geometries.

The brake cooling ducts were one of the most interesting parts of today's session because they introduced `completely new surface modeling techniques`. Unlike most of the previous components, which were created using solid modeling features, the brake cooling ducts relied heavily on Boundary Surface and Fill Surface. At first, I was confused about the difference between these techniques and the loft feature that I had used multiple times earlier in the project. However, I eventually understood that `lofting primarily focuses on creating a solid or surface by connecting multiple profile sketches`, while `boundary surfaces and fill surfaces provide more control over complex shapes by using guide curves and existing geometry`. These techniques were especially interesting because `aerodynamic components often require complex curved surfaces` that cannot easily be created through traditional extrusion methods. Understanding these features gave me more `confidence in my ability to model more advanced aerodynamic parts in future projects`.

The most meaningful part of today's session came while creating the wheels. Before beginning the wheel design, I noticed a detail that I felt was `inaccurate in the reference model`. The front wheel hubs included approximately 3 degrees of camber, while `the rear wheel hubs appeared to have no camber at all`. From an engineering perspective, this seemed unusual. In Formula One, `camber is a critical suspension setup parameter that influences tire contact with the track surface and affects grip during cornering`. I suspected that the simplified tutorial model had omitted this detail for convenience. Throughout this project, I have noticed that while the tutorial successfully creates the overall appearance of an F1 car, `certain mechanical details are simplified`. For example, some suspension components were not properly connected to the chassis, and the cockpit seat was represented more as a shape rather than an actual driver seat. These simplifications are understandable for learning purposes, but they also created `opportunities for me to improve the model by myself`. After `researching real Formula One suspension geometry`, I found that front wheels typically run higher camber angles compared to rear wheels. While the exact values vary depending on setup, `the front wheels generally have around 3–4 degrees of camber, while the rear wheels commonly use approximately 1–2 degrees`. Based on this research, I decided to `independently modify the rear wheel hub by rotating it approximately 2 degrees to introduce rear camber`. This was a particularly satisfying moment because it was `the first major improvement I made based on external engineering research rather than simple intuition or visual judgment`. Previously, many of my design improvements were based on what looked correct. This time, I was able to `identify a potential issue, research the engineering reasoning behind it, and implement a solution`. This was a small but meaningful step toward `thinking more like an engineer` rather than simply a CAD user.

After completing the mechanical components, I moved on to the exterior design stage of the car. The tutorial introduced techniques such as inserting text and using split lines to create patterns and surface details. While I found the design process extremely interesting, I decided to `postpone the livery and exterior styling until later`. My priority was to `complete the functional and mechanical aspects of the car first`. The exterior design is important visually, but it `does not contribute as significantly to understanding the engineering aspects of the vehicle`. I plan to create my own livery design later rather than directly following the tutorial's design choices.

The final challenge of today's session came while creating the wheels. Although the sketching, revolving, and circular pattern features were relatively straightforward, I encountered `unexpected difficulty with filleting`. Since I had already used fillets extensively throughout this project, I assumed this step would be simple. However, the challenge came from applying fillets to repeated circular geometry. The tutorial was able to group multiple edges together and apply the fillet efficiently, while I was `manually selecting individual edges`, which was much more time-consuming. Even after learning how to group edges, I found that `some unwanted edges were included while some were also missed`. This showed me that while I understand the basic concept of filleting, I still `have room to improve my efficiency when working with more complex geometry`. This is an area I want to explore further because `improving modeling speed and feature management will become increasingly important when working on larger and more complicated CAD projects`.

Overall, today's session was one of the most meaningful days of this project. I learned many new SolidWorks techniques, but more importantly, I began `taking ownership of the design process`. Instead of only asking "How do I recreate this?", I started asking `"Does this make engineering sense?"` That shift in mindset is something I value greatly because motorsports engineering is not only about creating visually impressive designs but is also about `understanding why a design exists and making informed decisions to improve performance`. At this point, the main structure of the F1 car is essentially complete. The remaining tasks are the wheel assembly and the livery design. Seeing the car transform from a basic chassis into a detailed Formula One machine has been incredibly rewarding, and I am excited to finish the final stages of this project.
