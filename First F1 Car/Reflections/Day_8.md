## 📌 Overview

Today's progress was `much slower` than I initially expected due to several challenges I encountered throughout the design process. Designing something that looks good on a sketch is one thing; translating that idea onto a complex, curved 3D surface is an entirely different challenge.

## 💭 Reflection

Today's session did not go exactly as planned, but it gave me an even `greater appreciation for how much creativity, technical skill, and attention to detail goes into designing a professional race car livery`.

As mentioned in Day 7, I started working on the livery design. But first, I decided to `create a preliminary concept rather than immediately designing directly on the SolidWorks model`. I felt this would be a more realistic approach to the design process. Similar to how engineers and designers often develop ideas through sketches, references, and iterations before committing to a final design, I wanted to `establish a general concept before translating it into CAD`. The image below shows the rough livery concept I created for my F1 car.

Although the concept may look relatively simple, there were actually `many different ideas and iterations behind it`. One of the main inspirations came from Porsche's design language. As mentioned in Day 7, I noticed that `Porsche frequently uses strong lines to emphasize the curvature and shape of its cars`. I particularly liked the use of the `two stripes along the hood of certain Porsche models`, so I decided to incorporate a similar concept into my F1 car.

I also wanted to introduce a more subtle design element on the monocoque. I created a line that would appear relatively normal when viewed from the side but `form a shield-like shape when viewed from above`. This was intended as `a subtle reference to the shape of the Porsche crest` rather than simply placing the logo directly onto the car.

The rear wing presented another challenge. I initially struggled to think of a design that fit the overall theme of the car. While researching historical and modern Formula One liveries, I noticed that some teams `prominently display their team name across the rear wing`. I really liked the visual impact of this approach, so I decided to incorporate my own version of it by placing `PORSCHE across the upper rear wing`.

`The most difficult part of the design process`, however, turned out not to be creating the individual design elements, but `deciding how to color the car as a whole`. I decided to use colors inspired by the Porsche crest, particularly `red, black, white, and gold`. While the gold accent lines were relatively straightforward to implement, `choosing a primary body color and deciding how the colors should interact with one another was surprisingly difficult`. Every F1 car needs a cohesive visual identity, and I quickly realized that simply having several colors I liked did not necessarily mean that they would work well together. After experimenting with different ideas, I eventually decided to `divide parts of the main body diagonally`, using dark red and gray as the primary colors. The black rear wing and halo, along with the white Porsche lettering, naturally complemented the main body. I also experimented with an alternating black-and-red design for the front wing. `Although the design still needs further refinement, this process gave me a much better appreciation for the work of professional livery designers`. The seemingly simple liveries seen on race cars can actually `require an enormous amount of thought and iteration to make every element work together`.

The biggest challenge today was not coming up with the design itself, but `translating the 2D concept into the SolidWorks model`. The Split Line feature worked particularly well on relatively simple surfaces, but became `much more difficult when I tried to apply it to the highly curved surfaces of the chassis`. The design sketches needed to intersect the target surfaces correctly, and even small differences in alignment could cause the split to fail. One of the most frustrating examples was the shield-shaped highlight line on the monocoque. I initially tried to convert the top view of the monocoque into a sketch so that I could use it as an accurate reference for the design. However, because the monocoque is a curved 3D surface, the resulting sketch did not naturally conform to the surface. This made it difficult to position the Split Line correctly. After experimenting with the sketch positioning and making several adjustments, I was eventually able to get the design onto the body. It was a frustrating process, but it reinforced an important lesson: `a design that works perfectly in 2D does not necessarily translate perfectly into 3D`.

This was also the reason why some of my original design ideas had to change. `For example, the PORSCHE lettering across both rear wing elements did not look as good on the actual model as it did in my initial concept`. Instead of forcing the original design onto the car, I decided to place the Porsche lettering only on the upper rear wing and introduce additional line elements to give the lower wing more visual appeals. Similarly, while experimenting with the double stripe on the nose, I realized that `a single line created a cleaner and more cohesive appearance`. I therefore decided to simplify the design rather than staying strictly committed to my original sketch. This was probably the most important lesson from today's session. `Design is not simply about executing an initial idea but is about continuously evaluating that idea and being willing to change it when the final result does not work`.

Overall, today's session was considerably more frustrating than I expected, but it was also one of the most valuable sessions for understanding the design process. Until now, much of this project has focused on learning SolidWorks techniques and using them to construct the car. Today was different. I had to `make decisions based on my own creativity, evaluate whether those decisions actually worked on the model, identify what was wrong, and modify the design accordingly`. Most importantly, I feel that the car is `finally becoming my own design` rather than simply a model based on a tutorial. The original tutorial provided the foundation for the car, but the livery, visual identity, and future modifications are increasingly becoming products of my own ideas and engineering judgment. Today's experience reminded me that `good design rarely happens perfectly on the first attempt`. It requires `experimentation, failure, refinement, and sometimes the willingness to abandon an idea that looked good on paper`. That is a lesson I hope to carry into future engineering projects far beyond SolidWorks.
