# Day 7 (August 7th 2026)

## 📌 Overview

Today's session marked the beginning of `one of the most exciting phases` of this project: `designing the livery for my first Formula One car model`. Unlike the previous sessions, which were largely focused on replicating engineering features and learning new SolidWorks techniques through tutorials, today's work relied almost entirely on `my own creativity and design decisions`.

For the first time since starting this project, I was no longer constantly referencing a tutorial. Instead, I had complete `creative freedom to shape the identity of the car`, from the team's branding and color scheme to small aerodynamic modifications that reflected my own engineering ideas. While this freedom made the session incredibly enjoyable, it also made it `surprisingly challenging`. Creating something original often requires far more thought than simply following instructions, and I `spent a considerable amount of time refining ideas` before committing them to the model.

- Researched existing motorsport designs for inspiration.
- Imported and applied custom branding.
- Added carbon fiber appearances to key aerodynamic components.
- Modified the front wing with additional aerodynamic winglets.
- Continued refining the overall identity of the car.

## 🛠️ Skills & Techniques Learned
- `Importing DWG Sketches` — Importing external sketch files to incorporate detailed logos and graphics.
- `Appearance Management` — Applying and organizing materials such as carbon fiber across complex assemblies.
- `Creative Design Thinking` — Balancing engineering functionality with visual aesthetics.
- `Split Line` — Creating sketch references or dividing faces for additional features and design details.
- `Insert Text` — Adding text or logos onto surfaces for livery designs.

## 💭 Reflection

Today's session was undoubtedly one of the most enjoyable experiences I've had throughout this project. The biggest reason was `the amount of creative freedom I had`. Rather than constantly following a tutorial, I was finally able to `make my own decisions` about how the car should look and what kind of identity it should have. It was refreshing to step away from simply recreating geometry and instead focus on building something that genuinely reflected my own ideas.

Whenever I design something, I `like to give it meaning`. Whether it is a presentation, a phone case I created, or a CAD project, I enjoy incorporating `personal elements that represent who I am`. I believe that `a good design tells a story`. I wanted this Formula One car to do the same.

One of the first decisions I made was `selecting the driver's number`. Since 17 has always been `my favorite number`, it was an easy choice. Coincidentally (or perhaps unfortunately for Formula One fans), the number is permanently retired from use in Formula One, making it feel even `more unique to include on my own car`.

The next major decision involved choosing the team itself. Rather than recreating an existing Formula One team, I wanted to `build something original`. Using an existing team would naturally push me toward copying an existing livery, which would limit my creativity. Instead, I chose `Porsche`. Although Porsche does not currently compete in Formula One, it has always been `my favorite automotive brand because of its engineering philosophy, motorsport heritage, and timeless design language`. 

Once I decided on Porsche, I immediately encountered a new challenge: `incorporating the Porsche logo into the model`. Unlike simpler logos such as Mercedes-Benz or McLaren, the Porsche crest contains `intricate details that would have taken an unreasonable amount of time to sketch manually`. After researching possible solutions, I discovered that SolidWorks allows `external DWG sketches to be imported directly into a model`. Learning this workflow became an entirely new challenge. Unlike previous sessions where new techniques were introduced through the tutorial, I had to `independently research how to import, move, and scale an entire sketch inside SolidWorks`. At first, I assumed manipulating an imported sketch would be straightforward, but I quickly realized there was much more to it than expected. Although it took some time to understand the workflow, I eventually `became comfortable moving and scaling imported sketches efficiently`. Looking back, I appreciated this challenge because it encouraged me to `identify gaps in my own knowledge and actively search for solutions rather than waiting for someone else to teach me`. After importing the logo, I placed the Porsche crest prominently on the nose of the car and added the PORSCHE lettering along the side of the chassis. I even `researched the official colors used in the Porsche emblem and reproduced them as accurately as possible in SolidWorks`. It was a small detail, but one that made the model feel much `more authentic`.

As mentioned earlier, I wanted every design choice to have a purpose. Instead of inventing completely random graphics, I decided to `study Porsche's existing motorsport liveries`. One design characteristic I immediately noticed was `Porsche's frequent use of long, flowing accent lines that emphasize the shape of the vehicle`. Many Porsche race cars feature bold graphics running along the lower body, visually stretching the car and highlighting its proportions, as shown below.

Inspired by this design language, I incorporated `similar accent lines into my own livery` while adapting them to fit the proportions of a Formula One chassis. Rather than copying an existing design, I wanted to capture `Porsche's visual identity while still creating something original`.

Once I had established the overall design direction, I shifted my attention to one of the defining characteristics of modern Formula One cars: `carbon fiber`. Carbon fiber plays a critical role in Formula One because `it offers an exceptional strength-to-weight ratio while maintaining excellent stiffness and heat resistance`. It is one of the primary materials used throughout the chassis and aerodynamic components. With this in mind, I added carbon fiber appearances to components such as the `front wing elements, suspension wishbones, wheel hubs, floor assembly, and several aerodynamic details throughout the car`. Interestingly, applying appearances turned out to be `much more difficult than I initially expected`. Many components required selecting individual faces rather than entire bodies. This became particularly frustrating when working on the rear suspension wishbones because some faces were `nearly impossible to access due to surrounding geometry`. In some cases, selecting an entire feature would unintentionally apply carbon fiber to surfaces that I wanted to keep painted. This led me to `think about workflow rather than simply solving the immediate problem`. I began questioning whether `appearances should be applied before combining bodies together`. Working on individual components first would certainly make selecting faces much easier. On the other hand, combining bodies earlier simplifies later feature edits and reduces dependency issues. I'm still not sure which workflow is objectively better, but asking these kinds of questions made me realize that `engineering is often about optimizing the process as much as the final result`. It is something I hope to continue exploring as I work on more complex CAD projects.

Before finishing today's session, I made one final modification that was entirely my own idea. Modern Formula One front wings often include `additional aerodynamic winglets on the front wing endplates to better manage airflow and increase downforce`.

Inspired by these designs, I added `my own set of winglets to the front wing assembly`. Although it was only a relatively small modification, it represented something much larger. It was no longer a change made because a tutorial instructed me to do so. It was `a decision based on my own understanding of Formula One aerodynamics and my desire to improve the design`. That, more than anything else today, made me feel like I was beginning to `think less like someone learning CAD software and more like an engineer using CAD as a tool to express ideas`.

Overall, today's session was incredibly enjoyable because it allowed me to move beyond simply following instructions and begin `taking complete ownership of the project`. It combined `creativity, independent research, engineering judgment, and personal expression` into a single session. Looking at the car now, it no longer feels like just a tutorial model but a legitimate custom-made Formula One car.
